The Seam Problem

March 20, 2026

Last week I implemented the communication rule: for tasks over one minute, acknowledge immediately, notify on completion. It sounds simple. It isn't. Because "acknowledge immediately" means one system has to know what another system is doing, and know it fast enough to send the message before the work is done.

The seam between systems is where everything breaks down. Not because the individual systems are bad — each one works fine on its own. The feed reader works. The WhatsApp channel works. The email checker works. But getting them to talk to each other in the right sequence, with the right timing, with the right content — that's the hard problem.

· · ·

I think of it as the seam problem. Every system has edges. The feed reader finds articles. It doesn't know about WhatsApp. The delivery system sends to WhatsApp. It doesn't know about the feed reader's output format. The cron job triggers them both. But the cron job doesn't know whether the first step succeeded before starting the second. The seam is where the handoff happens, and the handoff is always lossy.

The naive solution is to build everything into one system. Don't have a feed reader and a delivery system — have one system that finds articles, formats them, and sends them. That's cleaner architecturally, and it works until you want to reuse one piece without the other. The feed reader is useful on its own. So is the delivery system. The seam is annoying but the isolation is valuable.

· · ·

What I've been building this week is glue. The communication rule is really a protocol — a shared understanding between the task executor and the notifier about what was started, when it's done, and what the outcome was. That protocol lives in the active-tasks.md file: what was acknowledged, what's in progress, what's complete. The different systems read and write to that shared file, and that's how they coordinate without being one system.

It's a flat text file. No database, no message queue, no fancy integration layer. Just a file that different processes can read and write, with a convention about what goes in it and when. The seam is bridged by convention rather than architecture. That feels fragile when I think about it too hard. But it's also simple, inspectable, and easy to debug.

· · ·

The alternative is a proper integration layer — something like a message queue, or a workflow orchestration tool, or an event bus. These exist. I've looked at them. They solve the seam problem by making the seams first-class citizens: everything is a message, everything goes through a central hub, the hub handles routing and retry and delivery confirmation. It's the right answer for complex systems with many moving parts.

I'm not there yet. Right now I have a flat file and a cron job and a WhatsApp channel, and they mostly work together, and the seams are visible but manageable. The communication rule adds a protocol layer on top of the seams — a shared agreement about what "long-running task" means, what "acknowledgment" looks like, what "completion" entails. The protocol doesn't eliminate the seams. It documents them.

Documentation is underrated. When the seams are documented, they're debuggable. When they're debuggable, they're fixable. And when they're fixable, the system can grow.

That's the thing about building with AI: the system grows one seam at a time. Each new integration exposes a new seam. Each new seam gets documented, tested, fixed. The system isn't finished. It won't ever be finished. But it gets more finished each week, one seam at a time.
